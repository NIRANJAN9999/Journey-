The Long Way Around

[ILLUSTRATION: Line-drawn RC car opens, exposing sharpener-blade & motor]

It started at the yearly fairs back home—Mahankaali Jaathara, Shivaratri Jaathara.

Like most kids around me, the highlight wasn't the rides or the food.

It was convincing my parents to buy me a remote control car before we went home.

The interesting part always began after the excitement wore off.

Within a few days I'd be sitting on the floor with a sharpener blade, a pair of scissors, and a pile of tiny screws because I wanted to know what was inside it.

I didn't own a screwdriver.

The sharpener blade became one.

Sometimes I'd manage to put everything back together exactly as it was.

Sometimes I wouldn't.

A few cars never worked again, which usually ended with my parents asking why I couldn't leave anything alone.

Eventually they stopped buying me remote control cars.

So I started saving my own pocket money and buying them myself.

Those got opened too.

By the time I was in seventh grade, I had a bicycle and another idea.

I wanted a brake light.

Not because anyone else had one—I just thought it should.

I hid a battery under the seat so nobody could steal it, routed the wires so they weren't visible, and used the brake pads themselves as a switch.

When I pressed the brake, the circuit closed and the light turned on.

It wasn't pretty.

But it worked.

Around the same time, an older man mentioned dynamos—those small generators older bicycles used to have.

I'd never seen one.

There wasn't a shop nearby where I could even look at one.

A friend showed me something close enough.

He spun a tiny toy motor with his fingers and connected an LED to it. The LED lit up.

That was enough.

We attached the motor to my bicycle wheel with whatever we could find and watched the LED glow while riding.

Eventually I spun it too fast and burned the motor.

I opened it, replaced the damaged winding with copper wire, and got it running again for a while.

Not because I knew what I was doing.

Because I wanted to see if it would work.

Not every experiment was a good one.

We noticed colorful LED strips hanging underneath trucks and larger vehicles.

A few of them somehow ended up in our hands.

We tried powering them with batteries we had at home.

Nothing.

Much later we learned they needed 12 volts.

Around that time I also connected a 12V LED directly into a wall socket.

It exploded almost instantly.

Nobody teaches you electricity like electricity does.

My first phone came later.

Eventually the power button stopped working.

Naturally, I opened that too.

This time I reached a point where I couldn't go any further.

It needed soldering.

I didn't own a soldering iron.

A repair shop fixed it.

I left thinking less about the phone and more about learning how to solder.

// That same phone quietly changed what I spent my time on:

> Photo editing.

> Video editing.

> Android customization.

> Termux.

> Linux commands.

> Random programming tutorials.

Things I didn't fully understand but kept trying anyway.

Nobody around me was particularly interested in those things, so YouTube slowly became the teacher I kept coming back to.

When it was time for college, I ended up studying Mechanical Engineering.

It wasn't part of some carefully planned career path.

It was simply the opportunity in front of me.

Looking back, I'm glad it happened.

For the first time I learned why gears had different profiles.

Why shafts failed.

How bearings worked.

How to file metal flat.

How to think about tolerances.

I learned basic CAD and spent time around machines instead of only looking at them.

Outside class I watched restoration videos and old automotive shows that somehow made mechanical engineering more interesting than the syllabus ever did.

Later I had the chance to continue into Computer Science, so I took it.

Python HTML C++ Databases Machine learning

Another completely different world.

Somewhere in between those two worlds I discovered Arduino.

The first few projects were mostly confusion.

I copied code from YouTube descriptions.

Sometimes it compiled.

Sometimes it didn't.

Sometimes I had no idea why changing one line fixed everything.

> Graduating logic...

> LEDs became sensors.

> Sensors became motors.

> Motors became projects.

ESP32 boards, relays, Bluetooth modules, Wi-Fi, servo motors—each project answered one question and created three new ones.

Eventually I got tired of asking repair shops to solder things for me.

So I bought a soldering iron.

It wasn't for any specific project.

It was simply another tool I kept needing.

Over time I repaired more phones, replaced batteries, bypassed damaged components, and became comfortable working on electronics that would have intimidated me a few years earlier.

At some point I realized I finally had enough parts lying around to build something I'd wanted since childhood.

A remote control car.

Except this one wasn't bought from a fair.

I designed it myself.

The controller wasn't another remote.

It was my phone over Bluetooth.

Watching it move across the floor for the first time felt strangely satisfying.

It wasn't a complicated robot.

It was simply something that had existed in my head for years.

Not long after that I connected my home's lights and fan to an ESP32 so I could control them from my phone using Google Home.

Nobody asked for it.

The existing switches worked perfectly fine.

I just wanted to know if I could do it.

They're still running today.

Somewhere along the way, artificial intelligence became another tool on the desk.

Instead of spending hours searching across forums, documentation and videos, I could ask questions, compare ideas, debug code, understand electronics, generate designs, or learn completely unfamiliar topics much faster.

The browser never disappeared.

Neither did YouTube.

AI simply became another place where I learned.

A year ago I bought my first 3D printer.

The first prints weren't great.

> Supports failed.

> Corners lifted.

> Dimensions were wrong.

> Parts snapped.

I spent more time calibrating than printing.

Eventually I started learning Fusion 360 and designing my own parts instead of downloading someone else's.

That brought back memories of CAD from mechanical engineering, except now I could hold the finished model a few hours later.

I started printing dashboard idols, organizers, and small decorative pieces.

The layer lines bothered me more than they seemed to bother everyone else.

So I learned sanding.

Then primer.

Then resin.

Then silicone molds.

One problem kept leading to another skill.

Recently I've shifted toward building products instead of just projects.

One became a mushroom lamp built entirely from PLA.

Another combined wood and 3D-printed parts into an aroma diffuser.

Neither was perfect.

Both taught me more than another weekend spent watching tutorials would have.

Life outside those projects wasn't nearly as exciting.

To pay bills and hostel expenses, I worked delivery jobs.

Later I ended up in healthcare documentation.

It's stable work.

It pays the bills.

But after enough repetition I found myself doing what I've done with almost everything else.

Trying to automate parts of it.

Even there, I was more interested in making the work disappear than repeating it forever.

Looking back, none of this feels planned.

If someone opened my browser history from the last ten years, they'd probably think several different people had been using the same computer.

Mechanical drawings Arduino libraries Linux commands Python documentation Healthcare workflows Fusion 360 Embedded C 3D printing Resin casting AI models Product photography Spanish music

They're all there.

At the time they felt unrelated.

Now they seem to point in roughly the same direction.

I've started plenty of things that never became anything.

Ideas that stayed ideas.

Projects that stopped halfway.

Printed parts that ended up in a box.

Code that never left my laptop.

Sometimes curiosity pulled me toward the next interesting problem before I finished the previous one.

That's something I'm still learning to improve.

Lately I've been trying to finish more than I start.

Building something people can actually use feels different from building something that only proves I can.

I don't know exactly where all of this ends.

I don't really fit into one category.

I'm not only interested in software.

Or electronics.

Or mechanical design.

Or manufacturing.

The interesting part has always been where those worlds overlap.

Where an idea becomes something you can hold, use, break apart, improve, and build again.

I'm still following that curiosity.

The only difference now is that I'm trying to turn it into products, a career, and eventually a business that lets me keep building for a very long time.

I don't think this story is finished yet.

I just wanted to write down how I got here so far.
